The split

Two products, one substrate

Maps, missions, waypoints, telemetry and the safety layer are shared. Everything above them diverges. Build the substrate once and give each job its own foreground.

Design against the real loop

What the operator actually does

Neither operator spends meaningful time watching a robot drive. A UI whose centrepiece is a live map is optimised for the demo, not the job.

Inspection operator

MOST DAYS
  1. Morning: did the overnight runs finish?
  2. Work the findings queue — ~60 waypoints, 5 minutes.
  3. Escalate two or three items.
  4. Occasionally re-record a waypoint whose framing drifted.
~95% of their time is spent in a review queue.

Security operator

MOST NIGHTS: NOTHING
  1. Alert at 03:00.
  2. Phone in hand, half awake — see the clip.
  3. Decide: real or not?
  4. Take control, investigate, challenge over audio, dispatch.
  5. Log the incident.
Every tap between notification and hands-on is a design failure.
Cross-cutting

The safety furniture

These appear on every screen in the same place, always. Each exists because of a specific platform behaviour that causes accidents.

Authority strip

Four values, always visible

The App can preempt you silently at any moment. The same stick deflection is 1 m/s or 3 m/s depending on a speed level set ten minutes ago — and at high speed, yaw authority is cut to a third. This is the most dangerous hidden state on the platform.

Two buttons, never one

Abort ≠ E-stop

Abort is graceful — stop the mission, decelerate, hold position. Routine, recoverable, wanted 95% of the time. E-stop is immediate: the robot stops responding and lowers to the ground. Make them look nothing alike.

Hold-to-drive

Don't defeat the dead-man

The protocol already expires every move command after one second. Mirror it: sticks spring to centre, driving requires continuous contact. Never build a cruise or latched-velocity mode — it throws away the one safety property you get for free.

Not the safety case

The physical e-stop on the robot and the RC handset are the safety case. The UI e-stop is a convenience, and the interface must not imply otherwise.

Layouts

One thing is primary

The most common robot-UI mistake is a four-pane dashboard with video, map, telemetry and controls all at 25% and all too small to use. Decide what's primary per screen; everything else is a PIP the operator promotes with one tap.

Takeover

The security product, in one screen. Reached from a push notification in a single tap, with video already connecting and control already requested.

  • Landscape, two-thumb. Sticks sit in the bottom corners where thumbs rest.
  • Taking control mid-mission is one action that both pauses the mission and acquires control. Two steps here is the difference between a 4-second and a 20-second response.
  • The latency badge sits on the video, because that is where the operator's eyes already are. Degrade the speed cap when it goes amber.
  • Detection runs on the robot's Orin NX — the client receives boxes and alerts, never a raw sensor stream.
  • E-stop and abort are centre-bottom, reachable by either thumb, and deliberately unalike.

Mission monitor

Map primary, video demoted to a PIP — the inverse of the takeover screen, because here the question is where, not what do I see.

  • Route coloured by status — done, current, pending — is the most legible progress indicator there is. It beats a percentage bar because it also answers "where".
  • The battery line shows budget versus actual, not just charge. A mission that won't fit should be caught before it starts, not at 80% complete.
  • Geofences are drawn here but enforced on the robot. A constraint that only exists in the client isn't a constraint.
  • Clients get the 2-D occupancy grid and a pose. Two 96-line LiDARs at 10 Hz will never fit down a phone link.

Screen 03 · mobile, portrait · drag the handle

Findings triage

The inspection deliverable, and the screen the operator actually lives in. Designed for three seconds per waypoint.

  • A wipe beats side-by-side. For two photographs of the same physical scene, a draggable wipe catches changes that survive the sweep. Side-by-side forces saccades and misses small deltas — drag the handle and watch the gauge and the stain appear.
  • One waypoint at a time, full screen. Never a grid of thumbnails.
  • Three actions only. Swipe-right for OK, because that is the overwhelming majority.
  • Pose error is shown, always. If the robot parked 40 cm off, the comparison is parallax, not a finding — and the operator must never be asked to judge it.
  • A history scrubber across the last N runs catches slow drift: corrosion, a settling crack, a tank emptying over weeks.

Alert

The whole security product compressed into one card: what happened, where, and a single control that puts hands on the robot.

  • A short pre/post clip, not a live stream. Nobody is watching a feed at 03:00, and you can't afford to ship one continuously anyway.
  • The 157 TOPS on the Orin NX exists for exactly this — person detection, zone motion, door state, thermal anomaly if fitted.
  • Evidence is written once and never edited: disciplined timestamps, map pose plus RTK global coordinates, an append-only incident log, exportable as a bundle.
  • Two-way audio from the takeover screen resolves a large share of incidents without dispatching anyone — capability-gated, since only some units carry a speaker.

Screen 05 · desktop

Mission editor & map

Desktop earns its screen area on what mobile does badly: authoring on a map, reviewing findings at scale, and managing the map itself.

  • Capture waypoints get a tighter tolerance than transit — 6 cm versus 25 cm. That single requirement propagates all the way back into the localisation and controller work.
  • The selected waypoint shows its heading and capture cone, because framing is what makes run N+1 comparable to run N.
  • Low-confidence regions are a first-class map object. LiDAR does not see glass, and glazed partitions are everywhere in exactly the lobbies security patrols cover.
  • Per-step properties carry the whole observation state: lights, head angle, dwell, camera, mode.
Authoring

Record-by-driving is the feature to build first

The operator teleops the robot along the route and drops waypoints at the current pose. It needs no map-reading skill, every waypoint is provably reachable because the robot just reached it, and the operator frames each shot with their own eyes.

On drop, capture everything

The full observation state

Pose and map, head pan/tilt, camera and head/tail orientation, fill-light state, ambient lux, speed level and mode on approach — and the reference image itself, shot now, framed deliberately. Every future run is compared against it; everything else exists to reproduce the conditions it was taken under.

Platform affordances worth exposing

Things a generic AMR UI wouldn't offer

Head/tail reversal — reverse out of a dead-end rather than three-point-turning. Stair mode for multi-floor routes. Slim for 50 cm gaps, climb for platforms, crawl for low clearance. And dark patrol: LiDAR needs no light, so lamps become a tactical choice rather than a necessity.

Scope discipline

What not to build

✕
Full mission-graph editing on a phone

Record-by-driving covers the mobile case entirely. Complex editing belongs on desktop.

✕
Point clouds streamed to a client

Two 96-line LiDARs at 10 Hz will not fit down a phone link. Clients get the 2-D grid and a pose.

✕
A 3-D map view as the primary navigation UI

It demos beautifully and is harder to read than a 2-D grid. Offer it as an inspection tool, not the default.

✕
Auto-take-control on connect

Always an explicit operator action, and always show who currently holds control.

✕
Cruise or latched velocity

Defeats the one-second dead-man the protocol gives you for free.

✕
A unified inspection + security dashboard

The two jobs want different foregrounds. Forcing one screen makes both worse.
